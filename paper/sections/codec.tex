\section{Codec}
\label{sec:codec}

The codec takes the per-sample bounds $(\delta^-_i, \delta^+_i)$ and
produces a bit stream whose decoded values lie in the \emph{admissible
interval}
\[
  A_i = [\,x_i - \delta^-_i,\ x_i + \delta^+_i\,].
\]
A budget of zero (or NaN) forces the sample to be stored exactly, and a
budget of $+\infty$ leaves it unconstrained. The stream format does not
depend on the rules, and the decoder reads only the stream.

\paragraph{Quantizer.} The lossy mode is closed-loop DPCM (differential
pulse-code modulation) on a grid of step $q = 2^e$. The prediction for
sample $i$ is the previous \emph{reconstruction}, $p_i = y_{i-1}$. Because
the prediction does not use the original values, the encoder and the
decoder compute the same prediction and errors do not accumulate. The
reconstruction is $y_i = p_i + k_i q$, where the integer \emph{step count}
$k_i$ is chosen from the admissible counts
\[
  K_i = \{\, k \in \mathbb{Z} : p_i + kq \in A_i \,\}.
\]
This set is non-empty whenever $q \le \delta^-_i + \delta^+_i$. The stream
stores either $k_i$ or the difference $k_i - k_{i-1}$.

\paragraph{Blocks.} The series is split into blocks of 128 samples, and
each block is coded either losslessly with Gorilla XOR
coding~\cite{pelkonen2015gorilla} or with the quantizer above, using one
exponent $e$ for the whole block. The encoder picks whichever is cheaper.
Each sample of the block proposes a candidate exponent: the largest $e$
with $2^e \le \delta^-_i + \delta^+_i$, so that $K_i \ne \emptyset$ (for a
counter, $2^e \le \delta^-_i$). The encoder also tries the three next
finer grids, because on discrete data a smaller step can give a smaller
stream. For a counter the quantizer is the floor quantizer of
\cref{prop:rate}(b), which keeps the reconstruction below the data and
non-decreasing.

A sample is \emph{escaped}, that is, stored as a raw 64-bit value, if its
step count would exceed $2^{50}$ in magnitude or if its reconstruction
misses $A_i$ in floating point. This also covers samples whose interval is
too narrow for the block's grid. The encoder checks every reconstructed
value against its bounds, computed bit for bit as the decoder will compute
it, so the bounds hold for the actual floating-point output.

\paragraph{Lazy steps.} The \emph{nearest} choice
$k_i = \mathrm{round}((x_i - p_i)/q)$ spreads errors evenly in both
directions. This suits a symmetric bound, but wastes most of an asymmetric
one. For every grid the encoder therefore also tries the \emph{lazy}
choice, the $k \in K_i$ closest to zero (so $y_i = p_i$ whenever the
prediction is admissible) or closest to $k_{i-1}$. With one-sided bounds
the prediction often stays admissible for long runs of samples, and the
entropy coder codes such runs at a few hundredths of a bit per sample. Per
block, the encoder compares four strategies (nearest or lazy, combined
with coding counts or differences) by their exact coded size.

\paragraph{Entropy coding.} Every block decision and every integer is
coded with an LZMA-style adaptive binary range coder~\cite{martin1979range}
with 15-bit probabilities. An integer (a step count or a difference) is
sent as a zero flag, a sign and its bit length in unary. These symbols are
modelled in a context given by the magnitude of the previous integer.
Then come the bit just below the leading one, which is also modelled, and
the remaining bits, which are written raw. The models adapt quickly at the
start of a stream and more slowly as their counts grow, so short streams
also compress well. For the ablation we keep a fixed-width variant that
stores the integers of a block with the block's largest bit length.

\paragraph{Chunks.} Prometheus cuts head chunks at 120 samples, and each
chunk must decode on its own. The codec can code the series as
independent streams, at 3--4 bytes of overhead per stream. The budget is
still computed on the whole series, because windows cross chunk
boundaries. A counter chunk starts from the previous chunk's last
reconstruction as a floor. Otherwise the output could drop at a chunk
boundary, and \code{rate} would see a reset that is not in the data.

\paragraph{Verify and repair.} After encoding, the codec decodes the
stream and re-evaluates every protected condition with the reference
semantics. For every evaluation whose condition changed, it sets the
budget of the evaluation's support to zero and encodes the series again.
The loop terminates, because a support stored exactly gives the original
values. For \code{sum/avg}, three components sum in different orders: the
verifier uses a sliding sum, the reference evaluator sums left to right,
and Prometheus uses Kahan summation. Their results differ by at most
$2\gamma_{n-1}\sum|y_i|$ (\cref{sec:budget}). The verifier therefore
accepts an evaluation only if its aggregate is farther than this from
$\theta$ or its support is bitwise exact. The analytic budget keeps twice
this margin and has not needed a repair on any dataset. The loop makes the
guarantee hold end to end with respect to the reference evaluator.

\paragraph{Optimistic budget.} The bounds of \cref{cor:onesided} assume
that all errors take their worst values at once, while in a window
aggregate the actual errors mostly cancel. The encoder can therefore start
from $\kappa\delta^\pm_i$ with $\kappa > 1$. On every evaluation that
changes, it halves the budget of the support, stopping at the analytic
budget, and sets it to zero once it has reached the analytic budget. By
\cref{thm:budget}, a support within the analytic budget cannot change its
evaluation. The analytic budget is therefore both the sound starting point
for $\kappa = 1$ and the floor that bounds the number of rounds. Compression
Safeguards~\cite{tyree2026safeguards} correct violations in a similar way,
but our encoder corrects only the supports whose evaluations changed.
